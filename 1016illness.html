Module 10 · Illness

When Everyone Stayed Home — and Everything Broke

COVID-19 didn't just make people sick — it simultaneously disrupted every critical infrastructure sector that depends on human movement. As millions sheltered in place beginning in March 2020, a cascade of second-order consequences began playing out: cardboard piled on every curb, landfills struggled to keep pace, sanitation crews thinned by illness fell behind, and recycling programs in cities across America simply stopped.

Meanwhile, the travel industry essentially ceased to exist. U.S. domestic air passenger traffic fell 96% in April 2020. The cruise industry was ordered to halt all operations. Hotels sat nearly empty. And as people stopped moving, they started ordering — triggering an e-commerce surge that overwhelmed shippers, filled ports with 300+ waiting container ships, and sent freight rates up 500%.

This module examines how a pathogen — a public health emergency — simultaneously functioned as a critical infrastructure event, cascading through sanitation, transportation, and global supply chains in ways no emergency plan had fully anticipated.

Aviation Collapse
The worst crisis in airline history: U.S. domestic air passenger traffic fell 96% in April 2020. Airlines recorded combined net losses of $126.4 billion on $373 billion in lost revenue in 2020 alone. At least 64 airlines permanently closed 2020-2023. The industry had never experienced anything close to this in its entire history.

CDC No Sail Order
The CDC's emergency directive halting all cruise ship operations in U.S. waters — issued after the Diamond Princess outbreak showed how disease spreads catastrophically in enclosed environments. It shut down an entire $150 billion industry with a single public health order, leaving ships stranded with crews aboard for months. Nearly 80% of hotel rooms also sat empty simultaneously.

E-Commerce Explosion
Click to reveal
E-Commerce Explosion
Global e-commerce sales jumped 25-30% in 2020 as lockdowns crushed physical retail overnight. FedEx and UPS began sustaining "holiday peak" delivery volumes year-round — something never seen before. Amazon's share of U.S. parcel volume jumped to 21%, surpassing FedEx. The surge generated the cardboard wave and strained every link of the last-mile delivery network.

Port Congestion
By 2021, more than 300 container ships were queuing at ports worldwide waiting to unload. Container freight rates surged up to 500% in peak periods. Empty containers piled up in Asia as U.S. imports overwhelmed return logistics. The port of Los Angeles — the U.S.'s busiest — regularly had 100+ ships anchored offshore awaiting berth.

Click to reveal
JIT Supply Chain Failure
COVID exposed the fragility of "just-in-time" inventory management — the practice of ordering only what's immediately needed, holding minimal stock. When factory closures, port congestion, and worker shortages hit simultaneously, supply chains with no safety stock collapsed globally. The result: empty shelves for toilet paper, PPE, semiconductors, and food.

Infrastructure Interdependency

Every critical infrastructure sector shares the same vulnerability: human beings.

▸ Everyone Home = Everyone's Garbage at the Curb

When shelter-in-place orders took effect in March 2020, commercial waste plummeted — offices, restaurants, and retail stores shut down. But household waste exploded. Americans were home for every meal, every delivery, every discarded PPE item. According to the Solid Waste Association of North America, U.S. cities saw a 20% average increase in municipal waste nationally. In Chicago, workers were seeing up to 50% more garbage — immediately, with no additional equipment or staffing.

▸ Sanitation Hit Three Waves

PBS NewsHour documented that the garbage crisis actually struck in three separate waves: Spring 2020 (initial lockdowns and waste surge), late 2020 (sustained holiday delivery cardboard overload), and the Omicron surge in late 2021-2022, when so many sanitation workers called out sick that Nashville halted curbside recycling before Christmas with nearly no warning. Atlanta, Louisville, and dozens of other cities followed with temporary suspensions.

▸ Recycling Programs Cut — Recyclables Went to Landfills

Budget deficits caused by the pandemic — combined with worker shortages — squeezed recycling programs that were already financially marginal. Multiple communities cut recycling entirely: Rock Springs (WY), East Peoria (IL), Nashville, Atlanta. Even where recycling continued, budgets were forcing cities to make impossible choices between the sanitation services that constitute critical public health infrastructure and the budget constraints the pandemic imposed simultaneously.

▸ The Cardboard Mountain

E-commerce deliveries created a specific waste crisis within the larger one: the surge in corrugated cardboard. Residential curbside programs were designed for a steady, predictable flow of cardboard — not the sustained avalanche of boxes from millions of daily deliveries. When FedEx and UPS began sustaining holiday-peak volumes year-round, the resulting cardboard volume overwhelmed both residential recycling and available processing capacity at recycling facilities.

▸ The Worst Year in Aviation History

In April 2020, U.S. domestic air passenger traffic fell 96% year-over-year — the largest decline ever recorded. Airlines worldwide lost $373 billion in revenue and posted $126.4 billion in net losses in 2020 alone. At least 64 airlines permanently ceased operations between 2020 and 2023. The International Air Transport Association described it as "the worst crisis the industry has ever faced" — worse than 9/11 and the 2008 financial crisis combined.

▸ Cruise Ships: A Perfect Disease Vector, Instantly Recognized

The Diamond Princess outbreak — where the virus spread rapidly through a sealed ship — provided the world its first visible demonstration of COVID's contagion. The CDC responded with a No Sail Order that halted all cruise operations in U.S. waters, shutting down a $150 billion global industry. Ships sat at anchor for months with skeleton crews. The cruise industry didn't resume meaningful operations until well into 2021, and some lines never fully recovered.

▸ FedEx and UPS Ran at "Christmas Every Day"

As physical retail collapsed, e-commerce exploded — global online sales jumped 25-30% in 2020. FedEx and UPS, designed around predictable peaks and valleys, suddenly faced sustained holiday-season volumes every single day, year-round. Both carriers were forced to suspend service guarantees and implement COVID peak surcharges. Some high-volume shippers had their weekly pickup caps cut. Amazon built its own logistics network so fast it overtook FedEx by parcel volume in 2020.

▸ Ports: The Global Supply Chain Locked Up

By 2021, the compounding effects of factory closures, surging demand, labor shortages, and container imbalances created the most severe port congestion in history — over 300 ships queuing globally. At Los Angeles/Long Beach, more than 100 vessels waited offshore at peak. Container freight rates — normally $1,500-2,000 per box — surged to over $10,000-15,000, a 500%+ increase. Every consumer good that traveled by ship became more expensive, contributing to the inflation that followed the pandemic.
