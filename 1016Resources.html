Knowing Where to Look — and Whether to Trust What You Find

Vulnerability assessment is only as good as the sources it draws from. A threat analysis built on unreliable, outdated, or biased information can be worse than no analysis at all — it creates false confidence and misdirected resources.

This module focuses on two skills that apply to everything in this course: knowing where to find credible resources for CIP analysis, and knowing how to evaluate those resources for reliability, authority, currency, and purpose. Whether you are writing an after-action report, assessing a sector's vulnerability, or researching a current event, the quality of your thinking depends entirely on the quality of your inputs.

We will look at the key government and industry resources that CIP practitioners actually use, and then apply a structured framework for questioning and evaluating any source you encounter — in this class or in the field.

CISA — Cybersecurity & Infrastructure Security Agency
The lead federal agency for CIP, housed under DHS. CISA.gov provides vulnerability advisories, sector-specific risk frameworks, threat assessments, and tools for infrastructure owners. It is the primary government hub practitioners use for current threat intelligence and protective guidance.

ISACs — Information Sharing & Analysis Centers
Sector-specific organizations that provide a trusted channel for sharing threat data, vulnerability information, and incident reports between government agencies and private infrastructure owners. Every critical sector — water, energy, financial, transportation — has its own ISAC. Membership gives practitioners access to threat intelligence that cannot be published publicly.

GAO — Government Accountability Office
The independent, nonpartisan congressional watchdog that audits and evaluates the effectiveness of federal programs — including CIP. GAO reports are publicly available, rigorously sourced, and provide objective assessments of how well agencies are protecting infrastructure. They are among the most reliable secondary sources available to researchers and students.
🌐
Open Source Intelligence (OSINT)
Click to reveal
Open Source Intelligence (OSINT)
Publicly available information gathered from news media, academic research, government reports, social media, and websites. OSINT is the starting point for most vulnerability analysis — but it requires rigorous evaluation. Anyone can publish anything, and the volume of information makes it easy to inadvertently cite biased, outdated, or simply wrong material.

The CRAAP Test
Click to reveal
The CRAAP Test
A structured framework for evaluating any source: Currency (how recent?), Relevance (how applicable to your question?), Authority (who produced it and are they qualified?), Accuracy (can it be verified or corroborated?), Purpose (why was it created — to inform, persuade, sell, or deceive?). Apply this before citing any source in a CIP analysis.
🎖️
Source Authority
Click to reveal
Source Authority
The credibility and expertise of the entity producing the information. In CIP analysis, authority means asking: Does this author or organization have the qualifications, access, and track record to make reliable claims about this vulnerability? A blog post about power grid security carries very different weight than a CISA advisory or a peer-reviewed engineering study.
🔵
Confirmation Bias
Click to reveal
Confirmation Bias
The human tendency to favor information that confirms existing beliefs while discounting or ignoring contradicting evidence. In vulnerability assessment, this is dangerous: an analyst who already believes a particular threat is minor will unconsciously seek sources that confirm that view — and may miss a genuine emerging threat until it is too late to act.
📄
Primary vs. Secondary Sources
Click to reveal
Primary vs. Secondary Sources
A primary source is original firsthand data — an incident report, sensor readings, a government assessment written by those who conducted the investigation. A secondary source summarizes or interprets primary data — a news article, a textbook, a review essay. Understanding the distinction tells you how close to the original event the information is, and how many potential layers of interpretation (and error) stand between you and the facts.

Resources CIP Practitioners Actually Use

Source: CISA.gov / DHS / Government Accountability Office / National Academies / ISAC network documentation
CISA: The One-Stop Shop for Infrastructure Guidance

The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency (CISA) was established in 2018 under DHS as the nation's risk advisor for critical infrastructure. Its website provides sector-specific risk guidance for all 16 critical infrastructure sectors, vulnerability advisories, the National Infrastructure Protection Plan (NIPP), training resources, and real-time threat alerts. For any CIP research, CISA.gov is the mandatory first stop.

CISA.gov DHS.gov Ready.gov InfraGard.org
ISACs: Intelligence You Cannot Google

Information Sharing and Analysis Centers exist for every critical sector — the Water ISAC (WaterISAC.org), the Electricity ISAC (E-ISAC), the Financial Services ISAC (FS-ISAC), and many others. What ISACs provide cannot be found via a web search: threat intelligence gathered from member incidents, vulnerability data shared in confidence, and early warning alerts distributed only to registered members. When practitioners say "the sector knows something is coming," they often mean an ISAC has flagged it.

WaterISAC.org E-ISAC (Electricity) FS-ISAC (Financial) MS-ISAC (Multi-State)
GAO and Congressional Reports: Holding the Government Accountable

The Government Accountability Office (GAO) produces publicly available audits of how federal agencies implement CIP programs. GAO reports identify gaps, document failures, and make recommendations — often before those problems become public knowledge through an incident. Equally useful are Congressional Research Service (CRS) reports, which provide nonpartisan background analysis of CIP policy issues. Both are free, rigorous, and citable.

GAO.gov CRS Reports (via Congress.gov) DHS OIG Reports

How to Question What You Read

Source: Source evaluation frameworks / SIFT method / information literacy research / CIP analyst best practices
Currency Matters More in CIP Than Almost Any Other Field

A vulnerability assessment of the U.S. electrical grid from 2015 may be completely irrelevant to the grid as it exists today — with different technology, different threat actors, and different regulatory requirements. When evaluating a source, ask: when was this published, and has anything changed since that would affect its conclusions? In a field where new attack vectors emerge weekly, currency is not a courtesy — it is a requirement.

Purpose: Why Was This Written?

Not all sources are created to inform. A white paper published by a cybersecurity vendor has a financial interest in making threats appear severe. A government press release may understate vulnerabilities for political reasons. An advocacy group may cherry-pick data to support a policy position. Before citing any source, ask: who funded this, what do they want the reader to believe, and does their purpose affect what they chose to include — or exclude?

The Danger of Confirmation Bias in Security Analysis

In CIP analysis, confirmation bias is not just an academic concern — it has contributed to real failures. Before major infrastructure incidents, warning signs were often present but dismissed because they did not fit the prevailing assessment of what the threat looked like. Effective vulnerability assessment requires actively seeking out information that challenges your conclusions, not just information that confirms them. The goal is accuracy, not agreement with what you already believe.

Always Ask: How Close Is This to the Original Source?

When a news article says "experts warn" or "a study found," your first question should be: which experts, and which study? A secondary source that summarizes primary research has already made interpretive choices about what to include, emphasize, or simplify. For CIP research — especially when writing a vulnerability assessment or after-action report — trace claims back to their primary source whenever possible. The further removed from the original, the more cautiously it should be cited.
